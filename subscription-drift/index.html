Pricing noteApp subscriptions · 28 currencies · public data, 2023 to 2026

Set a subscription price once. Then 28 currencies move it.

A $9.99 monthly subscription priced at the same dollar value in 28 currencies on 2 January 2023 was worth anywhere from $3.73 to $11.51 by 6 November 2026. When taxes or exchange rates move a lot, the store updates the prices it set for apps and one-time purchases. It leaves subscriptions where they are, so the drift lands on the developer or on the customer.

How it works

The store fills in every price once. For a subscription, that is where it stays.

1

One price in.

The developer sets one price in one storefront’s currency. Here: $9.99 a month, in US dollars.

2

Every other price out.

The store fills in a comparable price for every other storefront, allowing for local tax and the exchange rate that day.

3

Then the rates move.

When taxes or exchange rates move a lot, the store updates the prices it filled in for apps and one-time purchases. Subscriptions are left out: changing what a subscriber pays is the developer’s call.

Below $9.99: the local price is now worth less in dollars, so each subscriber there earns the developer less.

Above $9.99: the local price is now worth more in dollars, so customers there pay more than the plan’s dollar price.

One currency, start to end: on 2 January 2023, $9.99 bought 187.06 lira. A price fixed there was worth $3.73 on 6 November 2026, a drift of −62.66%. Tax and the store’s price points change where a local price starts, not how far it drifts: both stay fixed while only the exchange rate moves.

The trade-off · each dot is one rule; a dashed ring means some of its rises would need consent

Against a yearly reset, the guardrail made 45 price changes instead of 84, cut average drift from 4.30% to 3.49%, and kept prices within 10% on 98.17% of currency-days instead of 88.68%. The plain 10% rule drifts least (3.18%), but 11 of its rises would need consent, 8 of them in lira.

Without the lira, the guardrail’s worst moment is −14.41% instead of −35.95%, and prices sit within 10% on 99.71% of currency-days. One currency is the whole weak spot, which is why the requirement below hands it to the developer.

04 · Tax

When a tax changes, the price stays and the earnings move.

The store’s 2026 notices list 10 VAT and GST changes. Earnings are figured after the tax comes out of the price, so at an unchanged price each change moved a developer’s earnings per sale the same day: from −16.667% in Morocco to +3.810% in Lithuania.

Finland and Lithuania: the reduced rate for news, magazines and books. Latvia: qualifying books and publications. The others: the standard rate.

For apps and one-time purchases in Mauritius, Morocco and the Republic of the Congo, the store moved prices 18 days later. Subscriptions are left out of those updates, so for a subscription the lower earnings hold until the developer moves the price.

05 · The guardrail

Written as a requirement an engineer can build from.

A drift watch for subscriptions. The defaults come from the numbers above, and every one is the developer’s to change.

Drift watch for subscriptionsRequirement, first draft · scope: auto-renewable subscriptions sold in more than one storefront
Problem
Subscription prices stay where they were set while exchange rates and taxes move. In this sample 10 of 28 currencies drifted more than 10% in under four years, and one tax change moved earnings per sale by −16.667% overnight. Developers find out from their payouts.
Users
Developers who sell a subscription in more than one storefront, and the operations team that answers their questions.
Inputs
Each storefront’s current price, the base price and base storefront, a daily reference exchange rate, and the store’s own tax notices.
Rule (defaults)
  • Flag a storefront when its price drifts more than 10% from the base price, either way.
  • Suggest the price that restores parity, on the nearest price point.
  • Rises: at most one per subscription in 12 months, at least 365 days apart, so subscribers get a notice, not a consent request.
  • Cuts: any time. They reach existing subscribers automatically.
  • If a rise would be more than 50% and more than the consent amount, split it: 50% now, the rest at the next allowed rise.
What the developer sees
One list of flagged storefronts: drift today, the suggested price, and whether subscribers will be notified or asked to consent. One action schedules the changes. Nothing changes without the developer.
Edge cases
  • A currency falling faster than one rise a year can follow (the lira here, −35.95% at its low): flag it for a developer decision instead of chasing it.
  • Regions where every rise needs consent: show the consent cost before scheduling.
  • A currency changeover, such as the lev becoming the euro in 2026.
  • A tax introduced where there was none: earnings fall overnight (Morocco, −16.667%).
  • Subscribers on introductory offers, who renew once more at their old price.
  • Prices kept for existing subscribers, and a base price that is not in dollars.
How we know it works
  • Storefront-days within 10% of the base price: 98.17% under this rule here, against 79.91% untouched.
  • Rule-driven rises that need consent: 0 here.
  • Subscribers kept through the first renewal after a rise, against storefronts not repriced.

Sources and method

Where every number comes from.

Exchange rates

European Central Bank euro foreign exchange reference rates, daily (ecb.europa.eu), file retrieved 8 November 2026, 4 January 2021 to 6 November 2026. A currency’s rate per dollar is its rate per euro divided by the dollar’s rate per euro. The 28 currencies are those on the store’s own currency list that the bank also publishes.

The store’s rules

The store’s public developer documentation, read 8 November 2026: the help page on pricing auto-renewable subscriptions (the consent rules, and that the store does not adjust subscription prices for tax or exchange rates), and the tax and price update notices of 29 January, 11 May and 27 August 2026.

Method

Drift on a day = the local price ÷ that day’s rate ÷ the launch price − 1. Price points, tax and rounding are left out: they set where a local price starts, not how far it drifts. The 12-month consent window is measured between price-change dates; in practice each subscriber meets a rise at their own renewal. Regions where every rise needs consent are not modelled. A price change decided on a day applies from the next business day, and the page follows the listed price: existing subscribers move to a new price at their renewal after the store’s notice period. Each currency counts the same in the averages.
